# NAMING_DISTINCTION_SKILL

## Purpose

Create distinctive, credible, memorable product names that do not sound AI-generated, generic, over-engineered, or copied from common SaaS and Web3 naming patterns.

Use this skill after the product concept is selected and sufficiently stable, but before final branding, repository renaming, domain selection, demo recording, public launch, or submission packaging.

## Core principle

A strong name should feel discovered from the product’s world, central object, user ritual, or defining moment.

It should not feel mechanically assembled from generic industry vocabulary.

The name does not need to explain the entire product.

The interface, tagline, and product description can explain the function.

## Default naming status

All names generated during ideation are provisional.

Use:

`WORKING_NAME_ONLY`

until the Naming & Brand Distinction Gate receives a PASS verdict.

Do not treat a working name as final merely because it already appears in:

- prompts;
- conversations;
- repositories;
- mockups;
- source code;
- contracts;
- screenshots;
- demo scripts;
- internal planning documents.

Before the gate passes, prefer neutral internal descriptors such as:

- build receipt tool;
- versioned scope tool;
- client-change record;
- project snapshot receipt;
- submission evidence viewer;
- delivery manifest;
- revision record.

## Automatic rejection patterns

Reject names built primarily from generic combinations such as:

- noun + Chain;
- noun + Flow;
- noun + Lock;
- noun + Proof;
- noun + Trace;
- noun + Vault;
- noun + Ledger;
- noun + Hub;
- noun + Forge;
- noun + Stack;
- noun + Sync;
- noun + Link;
- noun + Protocol;
- noun + AI;
- noun + Labs;
- noun + OS;
- noun + Base;
- noun + X;
- X + noun.

Examples of weak working-name patterns:

- ReceiptChain;
- ScopeLock;
- BuildTrace;
- ProofFlow;
- TrustLedger;
- VerifyHub;
- ProjectForge;
- DeliverableOS.

These constructions are not absolutely forbidden, but they should fail by default because they are highly collision-prone and often resemble AI-generated naming.

## Additional rejection conditions

Reject a name when it:

- sounds like a hackathon placeholder;
- sounds like a Solidity library instead of a product;
- could describe many unrelated SaaS products;
- relies on generic claims such as trust, proof, secure, verified, immutable, smart, or seamless;
- uses random vowel deletion;
- adds “ly,” “ify,” “io,” “x,” or “ai” without meaningful origin;
- imitates an existing crypto protocol;
- depends on gradients, glow, or logo effects to feel distinctive;
- is difficult to pronounce after reading it once;
- creates spelling ambiguity when spoken;
- has no meaningful relationship with the product;
- sounds like a consulting agency;
- sounds like an AI assistant;
- feels childish, gimmicky, or excessively futuristic;
- limits the product to the hackathon when reasonable post-hackathon growth is expected;
- sounds like a feature rather than a product.

## Required naming inputs

Before generating candidates, define the following.

### Exact user

Who uses the product?

### Painful moment

What happened immediately before the user opened the product?

### Transformation

What becomes clearer, safer, easier, more accountable, more credible, or more permanent?

### Central object

What tangible or conceptual object exists after the workflow?

Examples:

- receipt;
- edition;
- revision;
- imprint;
- mark;
- snapshot;
- docket;
- margin note;
- checkpoint;
- release;
- seal;
- manifest;
- record;
- handoff;
- amendment.

### Signature behavior

What is the one product behavior that should influence the name?

### Signature moment

What is the exact visible moment users and judges should remember?

### Product metaphor

Choose one coherent naming territory.

Possible territories include:

- publishing and editions;
- archives and catalogues;
- printing and imprints;
- tailoring and alterations;
- construction and inspection;
- cartography and landmarks;
- shipping and manifests;
- photography and negatives;
- field notebooks;
- postal marks;
- legal annotations;
- workshop tools;
- geological layers;
- provenance and collections.

Do not combine unrelated metaphorical territories merely to create novelty.

## Naming exploration routes

Explore at least five distinct routes.

### Route 1 — Existing real words

Use real words that acquire a relevant new meaning through the product.

### Route 2 — Physical objects and marks

Explore tools, materials, objects, marks, and processes related to the product’s central action.

### Route 3 — Editorial and archival language

Explore vocabulary connected to drafts, editions, revisions, imprints, records, annotations, catalogues, and publication.

### Route 4 — Founder-origin vocabulary

Explore words connected to the founder’s actual experience, recurring frustration, or moment that created the product.

### Route 5 — Subtle invented names

Invent only when the name:

- has a clear linguistic root;
- is pronounceable;
- is easy to repeat;
- has clear spelling;
- avoids fashionable startup suffixes;
- remains credible when spoken aloud;
- has an explainable relationship with the product.

Do not generate dozens of near-identical variants.

Generate enough candidates to explore each territory meaningfully.

## Evaluation criteria

Score serious candidates from 1 to 10 for:

- distinctiveness;
- relationship to the product;
- memorability;
- pronunciation;
- spelling clarity;
- visual character;
- emotional tone;
- professional credibility;
- high-end portfolio suitability;
- usefulness beyond the hackathon;
- compatibility with a concise tagline;
- resistance to AI-slop perception;
- collision risk;
- cultural suitability;
- bilingual suitability when relevant;
- flexibility if the product expands.

Apply explicit penalties for:

- generic Web3 vocabulary;
- excessive literalness;
- trend dependence;
- confusing pronunciation;
- similarity to existing software products;
- similarity to tokens or protocols;
- sounding like a consulting agency;
- sounding like an AI assistant;
- requiring a long explanation before it can be remembered.

## Preliminary collision check

For every finalist, perform a public collision check covering:

- exact-name web search;
- close spelling variants;
- existing software products;
- crypto protocols;
- tokens and blockchain projects;
- GitHub organizations;
- GitHub repositories;
- package registries when technically relevant;
- common domain availability signals;
- major social handle collisions;
- app marketplaces when relevant;
- likely trademark conflicts;
- negative or inappropriate meanings;
- pronunciation or meaning issues in English and French.

Label the result:

`PRELIMINARY_COLLISION_CHECK`

Do not claim legal trademark clearance.

A free domain does not prove that a name is distinctive or legally available.

## Spoken test

Read every finalist in realistic sentences:

- “I built this with ___.”
- “You can verify it on ___.”
- “Send me the ___ link.”
- “The latest version is recorded in ___.”
- “I added ___ to my portfolio.”
- “We used ___ to approve the change.”
- “Open ___ and check the receipt.”
- “The project is called ___.”

Reject names that feel:

- awkward;
- artificial;
- difficult to repeat;
- difficult to spell;
- unclear when spoken;
- excessively sophisticated;
- generic in context.

## Visual identity test

Test the name in:

- a browser tab;
- a mobile header;
- a GitHub repository;
- a smart contract explorer;
- a Contra portfolio card;
- a hackathon submission page;
- the opening frame of a demo video;
- an X or LinkedIn post.

Reject names that only feel distinctive when surrounded by:

- gradients;
- glowing effects;
- decorative symbols;
- elaborate logo treatment;
- generic AI illustrations.

## Distinction test

For each finalist, answer:

- Could this name belong to five unrelated products?
- Does it rely on a generic technology word?
- Does it sound invented by a naming generator?
- Does it reflect the product’s central object or ritual?
- Is it memorable without the logo?
- Can a judge recall it ten minutes later?
- Does it create a visual or emotional image?
- Would it still work after the hackathon?
- Does it sound credible on a client invoice or portfolio?
- Is the name stronger than the current neutral descriptor?

Reject candidates that fail the distinction test.

## Naming verdicts

Allowed verdicts:

- PASS
- PASS_WITH_RESERVATIONS
- REVISE
- FAIL_GENERIC
- FAIL_COLLISION
- FAIL_PRONUNCIATION
- FAIL_PRODUCT_FIT

Do not proceed to final visual identity, public repository renaming, demo packaging, launch materials, or submission branding on:

- REVISE;
- FAIL_GENERIC;
- FAIL_COLLISION;
- FAIL_PRONUNCIATION;
- FAIL_PRODUCT_FIT.

## Governance

Faadil retains final approval over:

- final project name;
- tagline;
- public repository name;
- smart contract display name;
- domain;
- primary visual identity;
- public launch naming.

Do not silently replace an approved name.

When later evidence reveals a serious collision, pronunciation problem, negative meaning, or product-fit issue, flag it immediately and return to this gate.

## Final rule

The strongest name is not necessarily the name that explains the technology most literally.

Prefer a name that captures the product’s character, central object, ritual of use, or memorable user moment while leaving room for the product to grow.
