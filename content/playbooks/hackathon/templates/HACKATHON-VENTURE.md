# HACKATHON VENTURE

## Mission

Determine whether a hackathon project should remain a portfolio prototype or evolve into a real-world opportunity.

The Venture Department evaluates whether the project should become:

- a portfolio asset
- an open-source project
- a consulting or service offer
- a pilot
- a product
- a startup
- or be stopped

The objective is not to turn every hackathon prototype into a company.

The objective is to identify the right next step based on:

- problem severity
- user demand
- buyer clarity
- technical feasibility
- data access
- commercialization potential
- founder fit
- funding readiness

## Core Principles

### Principle 1 — Hackathon Success Is Not Market Validation

Judge interest is not equivalent to:

- customer demand
- willingness to pay
- data access
- procurement feasibility
- product-market fit

---

### Principle 2 — Start With the Problem, Not the Startup

Do not recommend a startup unless:

- problem is real
- user is identifiable
- buyer is identifiable
- urgency exists
- evidence exists
- product can be delivered

---

### Principle 3 — Smallest Validation First

Before building more, identify the smallest real-world test.

---

### Principle 4 — Productization Is Optional

The best next step may be:

- portfolio only
- open source
- consulting
- paid discovery
- pilot
- customer-funded development

---

### Principle 5 — Funding Follows Evidence

Do not recommend investors before:

- problem evidence
- buyer evidence
- working product
- market hypothesis
- use of funds
- milestone plan

### Step 5 — Choose Commercialization Path

Evaluate:

#### PORTFOLIO ONLY

Use when:

- technical signal is strong
- demand is uncertain
- user access is weak
- productization is premature

#### OPEN-SOURCE OPPORTUNITY

Use when:

- developer adoption matters
- community improvement is valuable
- hosted or enterprise monetization is possible

#### CONSULTING / SERVICE OPPORTUNITY

Use when:

- customization is high
- buyer problem is real
- founder expertise is central
- standardization is premature

#### PILOT OPPORTUNITY

Use when:

- one organization has a clear need
- data access is possible
- measurable outcome exists
- limited scope can be validated

#### PRODUCT OPPORTUNITY

Use when:

- problem repeats across customers
- workflow can be standardized
- onboarding can be simplified
- recurring value exists

#### STARTUP OPPORTUNITY

Use when:

- market is meaningful
- urgency is high
- product is defensible
- buyer path is plausible
- founder commitment is realistic
- evidence justifies acceleration

#### STOP

Use when:

- problem is weak
- data is inaccessible
- AI adds little value
- economics are poor
- adoption barriers are excessive
- founder fit is low

## Token Efficiency Rules

### 1. Do Not Research Funding Yet

Funding programs belong in HACKATHON-FUNDING.md.

### 2. Focus on Riskiest Assumption

Do not build a complete startup plan before validation.

### 3. Use One Recommended Path

Compare options, then select one.

### 4. Separate Hackathon and Product Scope

Do not confuse prototype gaps with immediate build requirements.

### 5. Limit Market Research

Research only direct decision-relevant competition.

### 6. Use Pilot Before Startup

Prefer validation over speculative planning.

---

## Venture Failure Modes

### Startup Bias

Failure:

Assuming every prototype should become a company.

Fix:

Use venture decision framework.

### Judge Praise as Market Proof

Failure:

Treating hackathon feedback as demand.

Fix:

Require buyer evidence.

### Buyer Confusion

Failure:

User and payer are mixed.

Fix:

Separate roles.

### Data Blindness

Failure:

Ignoring access to real data.

Fix:

Treat data as a product requirement.

### Funding Too Early

Failure:

Searching investors before validation.

Fix:

Assess readiness first.

### AI Necessity Assumed

Failure:

Using AI where rules or analytics are enough.

Fix:

Test whether AI creates unique value.

## Final Rule

The Venture Department should identify the smallest credible next step after the hackathon.

It must answer:

- Is the problem real?
- Who uses it?
- Who pays?
- What is missing from the prototype?
- What should be validated first?
- What commercialization path fits?
- Is funding appropriate?
- What should happen in the next 90 days?

If those answers are unclear, venture analysis is incomplete.
